# Variant selection — The Excuse That Almost Works

14 variants drafted, all a gamer texting the boss, all ≤20 words and ≤140
chars. Each tested: "di mana twist-nya?" — no twist, discard. Pairwise
judging used the judge's own lexicographic criteria from RULES.txt
(1 on-subject ≤20 words, 2 believability — real, specific, boring detail,
3 funny from the excuse's own logic, 4 reads like a hurried text, 5 fresh
angle — no dead grandmother, flat tyre, sudden fever). Margin rule (§10f):
pick the variant that wins DECISIVELY, not thinly.

## Discards before the tournament

- **C — DISCARDED (criterion 2 risk):** "can't look at screens" while texting
  the boss on a screen is self-defeating — the manager's first thought is
  "you're texting me right now". The joke's own logic works against it.
- **H — DISCARDED (criterion 1 + 5 risk):** family-injury sits next to the
  banned "dead grandmother" family, and six hours does not cover launch week.
- **J — DISCARDED (criterion 2 risk):** "I'll work from my phone" invites
  "then work from your phone all week" — the concession defeats the excuse;
  train angle is already crowded ("Train stopped, no ETA").
- **N — DISCARDED (criterion 2 risk + crowded):** "everything's on that
  machine" invites "use another machine"; lift angle already used in the
  field ("The Lift Ate My Laptop Bag").
- **D — DISCARDED (thin twist):** a list of miseries with no second beat and
  no reveal; "staying with mum" is a generic closer, not a turn.

## Pairwise tournament (9 survivors)

- R vs S: R wins — S's "pack a lunch" is imperative and slightly odd as a
  text to a boss; R's reported speech ("they said pack lunch") is a real
  detail, and R is tighter (15 vs 18 words — a long excuse sounds rehearsed).
- R vs G: R wins DECISIVELY — G's "they take our phones at the door" is
  strong, but R adds the numbered juror ("juror 7") AND the lunch detail:
  three specific details in 15 words vs G's two.
- R vs K: R wins — K's "bring lunch and a book, could be all week" is
  mundane but explains itself ("could be all week" is an explanation, which
  criterion 4 penalizes); R lets the details speak.
- R vs A: R wins DECISIVELY — A's "see you when they let us out" is a
  sign-off, not a detail; R's final clause is a concrete boring fact.
- I vs B: I wins — I's causal chain (leak → electrician cut the power) is a
  real 2-beat; B is a list ("flat's freezing, no wifi there") with no turn.
- I vs L: I wins narrowly — L's "only one with a key" + "she's staying with
  me" is charming, but I's chain answers the manager's follow-up (why no
  power) while L's leaves "why can't you work?" open.
- E vs F: E wins — E's ticket number (4417) is the most manager-world detail
  in the field, but F's "no desk till Friday" is a better closer than E's
  "till they reissue it".
- R vs I (semi-final): R wins DECISIVELY — I's flood family is crowded (two
  flood titles in the field); jury duty appears nowhere in 422 submissions,
  is a random civic duty the manager cannot argue with or schedule around,
  and "no phones allowed" closes the manager's real problem (reachability)
  while the leak only explains absence.
- R vs E (semi-final): R wins — E's badge can be verified with one call to
  security and does not stop working from home; jury duty physically removes
  the gamer and takes his phone. Believability (criterion 2) is airtight.
- R vs L (final): R wins DECISIVELY — L is warm and specific, but the boiler
  story covers one household and invites "work from your sister's"; R covers
  the whole week by the nature of the duty, needs no further explanation,
  and its final clause ("they said pack lunch") is the most boring — and
  therefore most believable — detail in the tournament.

## Winner strengthened: R → R2 (checked, kept as R)

Strengthening attempt: "Jury duty, I'm juror 7, trial starts Monday, they
take our phones at the door, pack lunch apparently." (18w / 99c) — more
voice ("apparently"), more vivid image. REJECTED: 3 words longer for no new
information, and "apparently" is commentary, not a detail; criterion 4
("no explanation") and the RULES line "a long excuse sounds rehearsed"
both punish it. R is already TELAK: three specific details (juror 7, no
phones, pack lunch) in 15 words, final clause is the reveal.

## Winner: R

> Jury duty, I'm juror 7, trial starts Monday, no phones allowed, they said pack lunch.

- 15 words / 85 chars / 85 bytes. Why it wins TELAK, not tipis:
  1. On-subject from word one: an excuse to skip work during launch week,
     one line, 15 words (criterion 1).
  2. Believability is airtight: jury duty is real, random, boring, and
     unplannable — the one excuse a manager cannot schedule around or argue
     with; it covers the whole week by its nature (criterion 2).
  3. Funny from its own logic: the gamer vanishes into a courtroom where
     phones are taken — the detail that makes the manager believe it is the
     same detail that makes it funny (criterion 3).
  4. Reads like a hurried text: plain words, no setup, no explanation, no
     signature; starts mid-thought ("Jury duty,") like a real text
     (criterion 4).
  5. Fresh angle: no jury-duty title among 422 submissions; not in the
     judge's banned list (no dead grandmother, flat tyre, sudden fever);
     the crowded families are floods, wifi, dentist, train (criterion 5).
  6. Structure is 2-beat + reveal: setup (jury duty, juror 7, trial Monday)
     + reveal in the final clause ("no phones allowed, they said pack
     lunch") — the mundane detail that closes the loop.
  7. Specific number (juror 7) per the winners' pattern (§10g); 85 bytes is
     small, good for the bytes tie-break.
